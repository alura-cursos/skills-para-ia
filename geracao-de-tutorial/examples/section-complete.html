4. KL divergence — how different are two distributions?

Setup Distributions KL divergence Cross-entropy Forecasting

What you'll learn: KL divergence measures how far one probability distribution is from another. It's asymmetric (D(P‖Q) ≠ D(Q‖P)) and it shows up everywhere — scoring a forecast against reality, measuring information gain, data compression, hypothesis testing. After this section you'll know how to compute it, when to use it, and why it has the "covering vs mode-seeking" behavior people warn about.

Suppose you have two probability distributions over the same set of outcomes. Maybe P is the actual observed distribution of demand across five product categories, and Q is last quarter's forecast for those same categories. You want to know: how different are they?

A natural answer would be "the difference between the two probability vectors, element by element" — but that ignores the SCALE of probabilities. A 0.01 difference at probability 0.99 is much smaller (relatively) than a 0.01 difference at probability 0.01.

KL divergence answers this question in the right scale. It weights each outcome's contribution by how much P cares about that outcome (its probability) and measures the log-ratio of the two probabilities there. Read DKL(P‖Q) as the extra surprise the forecast Q costs you versus already knowing the true demand P. The result is in "nats" if you use natural log, "bits" if you use log base 2.

The geometry is asymmetric. If P has mass somewhere Q doesn't, the divergence is huge (you "can't get there from here" under Q). If P has zero mass where Q has mass, it's cheap — P just doesn't care. This asymmetry is the source of the "mode-seeking" vs "covering" behavior you'll hear about.

The formula. The KL divergence from P to Q over a discrete outcome space is:

\[ \textcolor{#ec4899}{D_{\text{KL}}}(\textcolor{#7aa2f7}{P} \,\|\, \textcolor{#7aa2f7}{Q}) = \sum_{i} \textcolor{#7aa2f7}{P}(\textcolor{#10b981}{i}) \, \log \frac{\textcolor{#7aa2f7}{P}(\textcolor{#10b981}{i})}{\textcolor{#7aa2f7}{Q}(\textcolor{#10b981}{i})} \]
  • DKL — the divergence; the quantity we compute. Units: nats (natural log) or bits (log2).
  • P, Q — two probability distributions over the same outcome space (here, observed demand and the forecast).
  • i — index over outcomes (here: product categories A, B, C, D, E).
  • The weight P(i) — each category's log-ratio contribution is weighted by how much observed demand P puts there. P puts 40% mass on B → B's log-ratio gets a 0.40 multiplier.
  • The log-ratio log(P(i) / Q(i)) — measures the relative excess of P over Q at outcome i. Positive when P > Q; negative when P < Q.

In plain words: for each outcome, take how much P cares about it (its probability), multiply by the log of how much P prefers it relative to Q, and sum across all outcomes. If P and Q agree everywhere, the log is zero everywhere, and KL = 0. If they disagree, the worst disagreement (weighted by P) dominates.

Worked example: DKL(P‖Q) for the distributions in Figure 4

Given observed demand P = [0.05, 0.40, 0.30, 0.20, 0.05] and forecast Q = [0.10, 0.10, 0.40, 0.30, 0.10] over product categories A–E (each vector sums to 1):

Compute each per-outcome contribution P(i) × log(P(i) / Q(i)):

  • A: 0.05 × log(0.05 / 0.10) = 0.05 × log(0.5) = 0.05 × (−0.693) = −0.0347
  • B: 0.40 × log(0.40 / 0.10) = 0.40 × log(4) = 0.40 × (+1.386) = +0.5545
  • C: 0.30 × log(0.30 / 0.40) = 0.30 × log(0.75) = 0.30 × (−0.288) = −0.0863
  • D: 0.20 × log(0.20 / 0.30) = 0.20 × log(0.667)= 0.20 × (−0.405) = −0.0811
  • E: 0.05 × log(0.05 / 0.10) = 0.05 × log(0.5) = 0.05 × (−0.693) = −0.0347

Sum: −0.0347 + 0.5545 + (−0.0863) + (−0.0811) + (−0.0347) = 0.3177 nats

So DKL(P‖Q) ≈ 0.317 nats of extra surprise the forecast costs. Note that B alone contributes 0.55 — more than the entire sum, because the other four contributions are negative (the forecast Q exceeds observed demand P there) and partially cancel. KL divergence is non-negative as a sum, but its per-outcome contributions can be either sign.

Try the asymmetry yourself: compute DKL(Q‖P). You'll get a different number (~0.31). The two are NOT interchangeable.

Takeaway. KL divergence is weighted log-disagreement — each outcome's contribution scales with how much P cares about it. That's why "P has mass where Q doesn't" is catastrophic (huge positive contribution) while "Q has mass where P doesn't" is free (no contribution).

You'll see this asymmetry again in §[N+1] (cross-entropy, which splits into the entropy of P plus DKL(P‖Q) — the forecast paying when it puts probability where demand doesn't), and again in §[N+2] (forecast scoring, where the per-category log-ratios here become the penalty you accumulate over time).
