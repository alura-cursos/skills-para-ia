Worked example: quicksort on [3, 6, 1, 8, 2, 4]

Input: arr = [3, 6, 1, 8, 2, 4]. Pivot strategy: last element. Initial call: quicksort(arr, lo=0, hi=5).

4 quicksort(arr, 2, 2) [3] — — —
5 quicksort(arr, 4, 5) (right of step-1 pivot) [8, 6] 6 (last) [6, 8] 4

Result. Final array [1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8] — sorted ✓. 6 partition operations on an N=6 input. Total comparisons: ~12 (each partition does N comparisons). Worst-case for quicksort is O(N²), but on this small balanced input we get O(N log N) ≈ 6 × log₂(6) ≈ 15.5 comparisons, which is close to what we observed.

Try this: trace quicksort on [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] (already sorted, pivot = last). You'll get the worst case: every partition leaves all-but-one element on one side, giving N² total comparisons. This is why random pivot selection matters in practice.
